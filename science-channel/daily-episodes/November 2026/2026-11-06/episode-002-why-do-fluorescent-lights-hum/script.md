# Why do fluorescent lights hum?

An engaging 8-10 minute science explainer exploring the physics behind the classic fluorescent light hum, connecting alternating current, gas-discharge physics, and magnetic ballast vibration.

## Scene 01

Have you ever stood in a quiet office or an old school hallway and heard that unmistakable, low-pitched buzzing sound right above your head?

## Scene 02

It is a sound so familiar, so universally tied to institutional spaces, that we often tune it out entirely as background noise.

## Scene 03

That subtle, persistent drone is the classic hum of the fluorescent light. But have you ever stopped to wonder what is actually making that noise?

## Scene 04

Is it the electricity itself? Is it the glass tube vibrating? Or is there something hidden away inside the metal fixture doing the work?

## Scene 05

To solve this acoustic mystery, we need to journey deep inside the circuitry of the light and look at how alternating current actually works.

## Scene 06

Unlike the steady direct current coming from your smartphone battery, wall outlets pump out alternating current, or AC.

## Scene 07

In North America, this current reverses direction sixty times every single second. In Europe and many other regions, it does it fifty times.

## Scene 08

We measure this reversing rate in Hertz. So standard household electricity operates at a frequency of sixty Hertz.

## Scene 09

Now, an incandescent light bulb simply doesn't care about this rapid flipping. Current goes left, current goes right, the filament stays hot and glows.

## Scene 10

Fluorescent lights, however, are an entirely different breed of technology. They do not use hot glowing metal wires to make their light.

## Scene 11

Instead, they are gas-discharge tubes. Inside that sealed glass cylinder is a carefully measured trace amount of mercury vapor and inert argon gas.

## Scene 12

When you flip the wall switch, electricity arcs across the tube, exciting the mercury atoms and causing them to emit invisible ultraviolet light.

## Scene 13

That invisible ultraviolet light then strikes the white phosphor coating painted on the inside of the glass, which fluoresces and glows visibly.

## Scene 14

Here is the catch: unlike a resistor, a fluorescent tube exhibits negative resistance. Once the gas starts conducting electricity, resistance drops drastically.

## Scene 15

If you hooked a fluorescent tube directly to a standard wall outlet without any intervention, it would draw infinite current and literally explode.

## Scene 16

Enter the unsung hero of the lighting fixture: the ballast. This essential device sits hidden inside the metal housing right behind the tube.

## Scene 17

Traditional magnetic ballasts are essentially heavy electromagnetic transformers. They consist of tightly wound copper coils wrapped around a solid core of laminated steel plates.

## Scene 18

The ballast has two main jobs. First, it provides a high-voltage spike to start the arc. Second, it limits and regulates the operating current.

## Scene 19

It is this magnetic ballast, and specifically the way it interacts with that sixty-Hertz alternating current, that creates our famous hum.

## Scene 20

Let's dive into the microscopic physics of what happens inside that metal core when the AC electricity starts flowing through the copper wire.

## Scene 21

Every time the electrical current surges in one direction, it builds up a magnetic field inside the steel core. Then, the current drops to zero.

## Scene 22

Then the current reverses direction, and the magnetic field builds up again in the opposite polarity. This happens sixty times every second.

## Scene 23

This constant magnetic reversal creates a fascinating physical phenomenon known to physicists and engineers as magnetostriction.

## Scene 24

Magnetostriction is the property of magnetic materials where their physical shape or dimensions change slightly in response to a magnetic field.

## Scene 25

As the magnetic field surges and reverses sixty times a second, the steel plates in the ballast literally expand and contract right along with it.

## Scene 26

Because the electrical current cycles at sixty Hertz, the magnetic field peaks one hundred and twenty times every second.

## Scene 27

That is because a peak occurs during both the positive half and the negative half of the alternating current wave cycle.

## Scene 28

So the steel core physically pulses one hundred and twenty times every second. That rapid mechanical pulsing pushes against the surrounding air.

## Scene 29

When air molecules are pushed back and forth at a rate of one hundred and twenty cycles per second, they generate an audible sound wave.

## Scene 30

In the realm of acoustics, one hundred and twenty Hertz sits squarely in the lower-middle register of the human hearing range.

## Scene 31

It is roughly equivalent to a low B on a piano keyboard. That is the exact fundamental frequency of the classic fluorescent hum.

## Scene 32

But wait, there is another physical force at play inside that ballast housing, compounding the acoustic vibrations even further.

## Scene 33

It is called magnetic attraction, or the Lorentz force. The individual coils of copper wire experience physical forces pulling on them as current flows.

## Scene 34

If any of those copper windings, steel laminations, or mounting screws are even slightly loose, they will physically rattle against each other.

## Scene 35

This mechanical rattling adds higher-frequency harmonics to the base one-hundred-and-twenty-Hertz tone, creating a richer, harsher buzzing texture.

## Scene 36

Furthermore, the entire metal housing of the ceiling fixture acts like an acoustic amplifier, much like the wooden body of an acoustic guitar.

## Scene 37

The hollow metal box captures the micro-vibrations of the ballast and radiates them efficiently into the room as a loud, room-filling drone.

## Scene 38

Now, you might wonder: do the glass tubes themselves contribute to this annoying acoustic phenomenon in any way?

## Scene 39

While the electrical discharge inside the tube does cycle at that same sixty-Hertz rate, the glass tube is generally too rigid to buzz loudly.

## Scene 40

However, cheap or old tubes can sometimes exhibit faint acoustic ringing if their internal components or cathode shields are slightly loose.

## Scene 41

For decades, lighting engineers tried to combat this annoying ballast hum by potting the internal components in solid asphalt or insulating resin.

## Scene 42

Encapsulating the coils in a solid block helps lock the steel laminations in place, drastically reducing magnetostriction rattling and quieting the fixture.

## Scene 43

The ultimate solution, however, arrived with the invention of the electronic ballast, revolutionizing modern commercial and residential lighting design.

## Scene 44

Instead of operating at sluggish sixty-Hertz mains frequency, electronic ballasts use solid-state circuitry to skyrocket the operating frequency.

## Scene 45

They drive fluorescent tubes at frequencies between twenty thousand and forty thousand Hertz, well above the upper limit of human hearing.

## Scene 46

Because human ears cannot perceive vibrations above twenty thousand Hertz, any magnetostriction occurring in these modern ballasts is completely silent to us.

## Scene 47

So the next time you hear that classic low-frequency hum in an older building, you will know you are listening to sixty-Hertz AC electricity at work.

## Scene 48

It is a brilliant physical symphony of electromagnetic fields, magnetostriction, and vibrating steel, all working together to light up our world.
