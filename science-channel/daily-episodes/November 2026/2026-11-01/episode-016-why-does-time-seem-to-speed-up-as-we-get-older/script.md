# Why does time seem to speed up as we get older?

An engaging 8-10 minute science explainer exploring the cognitive and neurological reasons behind the subjective acceleration of time as we age.

## Scene 01

Have you ever wondered why summer vacations felt like endless epochs when you were a child, but entire years flash by in the blink of an eye today?

## Scene 02

You are definitely not alone in experiencing this phenomenon. People across generations and cultures report this exact same bizarre sensation as they grow older.

## Scene 03

Time is an absolute constant for physicists, ticking forward at precisely one second per second. But for our brains, it is delightfully, maddeningly flexible.

## Scene 04

Why does our subjective perception of time warp so drastically? Is it a trick of memory, a biological slowdown, or something else entirely?

## Scene 05

To unravel this everyday mystery, we have to look into the fascinating intersection of human psychology, memory formation, and neurology.

## Scene 06

Let us start with one of the most mathematically intuitive explanations: the proportional theory of time perception, first proposed by philosopher Paul Janet.

## Scene 07

Imagine you are five years old. A single year represents a massive twenty percent of your entire conscious existence on this planet.

## Scene 08

That year feels monumental because it is a huge fraction of everything you have ever experienced. It is a vast, expansive era.

## Scene 09

Now fast-forward to when you turn fifty years old. That same single year is now a mere two percent of your lived experience.

## Scene 10

Mathematically speaking, each year naturally shrinks in relative proportion to the total accumulated length of your past life.

## Scene 11

While this proportional scaling offers a neat numerical baseline, it doesn't fully capture the rich, messy reality of how our brains actually process time.

## Scene 12

To understand the deeper mechanism, we must explore how memories are encoded, stored, and later retrieved by our cognitive machinery.

## Scene 13

Psychologist William James noted back in the nineteenth century that when our lives are filled with novelty, time seems to stretch out.

## Scene 14

Think about your first day at a brand-new school, or traveling to a completely foreign country for the very first time in your life.

## Scene 15

Every single detail is fresh, unexpected, and demanding of your attention. Your brain has to work overtime to process all this new sensory data.

## Scene 16

Because you are recording so many unique, distinct memories, looking back at that period makes it feel remarkably long and deeply rich.

## Scene 17

Now, consider the routine of adulthood. We commute along the exact same streets, work familiar jobs, and follow predictable daily habits.

## Scene 18

When your brain encounters familiar environments and routines, it goes into a sort of cognitive autopilot to save precious metabolic energy.

## Scene 19

It stops actively recording detailed new episodic memories because it thinks, 'I've seen this all before, no need to waste storage space.'

## Scene 20

When you look back over your year from this perspective, you find very few distinct mental milestones anchoring your memories.

## Scene 21

With fewer memories stored for a given span of time, your brain assumes that less time must have actually passed. It is an illusion of retrospection.

## Scene 22

This is often called the holiday paradox. A two-week vacation feels agonizingly long while you are on it, but vanishes instantly when you return.

## Scene 23

While you are there, novelty reigns supreme. But once you are home, those memories compress into a single summarized event in your mind.

## Scene 24

Beyond psychology and memory, neuroscientists also point to the physical hardware of our bodies: our central nervous system.

## Scene 25

Think of the brain as a biological computer processing frames of visual and sensory information, much like a high-speed digital camera.

## Scene 26

Children have smaller brains, shorter neural pathways, and extraordinarily fast metabolic rates. Their nervous systems process more frames per second.

## Scene 27

Because children process a higher density of neural frames, they literally experience more internal 'moments' packed into every single external clock second.

## Scene 28

As we age, our brains grow larger and our neural pathways become longer, thicker, and more myelinated with fatty insulation sheaths.

## Scene 29

While myelination helps signals travel efficiently, it also slightly increases the time it takes for electrical impulses to traverse complex networks.

## Scene 30

Furthermore, aging neurons accumulate minor wear and tear, and the brain's baseline processing speed gradually slows down over the decades.

## Scene 31

If your brain processes fewer frames per second as an adult, external time appears to move faster relative to your internal processing speed.

## Scene 32

It is remarkably similar to how smaller animals, like flies or mice, perceive our world as if it were moving in slow motion.

## Scene 33

A fly's visual flicker-fusion rate is vastly superior to ours, allowing it to process fast-paced environmental cues with incredible precision.

## Scene 34

As humans age, our internal frame rate drops, making the world outside seem to accelerate just like a projector speeding up.

## Scene 35

So, we have proportional scaling, memory compression from routine, and the physical slowdown of neural processing rates combining together.

## Scene 36

It is a triple-whammy that conspires to make our adult years feel like they are flying by at an increasingly relentless pace.

## Scene 37

Does this mean we are entirely helpless passengers trapped in a runaway train of accelerating subjective time? Absolutely not.

## Scene 38

Because we now understand the underlying mechanisms, we can actively hack our brains to slow down our perception of time.

## Scene 39

The secret weapon against the acceleration of time is novelty, mindfulness, and the deliberate disruption of our daily routines.

## Scene 40

You can force your brain out of autopilot by constantly learning new skills, picking up unfamiliar hobbies, or studying a new language.

## Scene 41

Travel to new places, even if it is just exploring a neighborhood in your own city that you have never bothered to visit before.

## Scene 42

Break your habits. Take a different route to work, rearrange your living room furniture, or cook recipes from a culture you know nothing about.

## Scene 43

By flooding your hippocampus with fresh, unexpected sensory inputs, you encourage your brain to wake up and start recording memories again.

## Scene 44

Practicing mindfulness is another powerful tool, anchoring your awareness firmly in the present moment rather than rushing ahead to the future.

## Scene 45

When you pay deep, undivided attention to the texture of your food, the sound of rain, or the warmth of the sun, time expands gracefully.

## Scene 46

Time may be an unyielding physical river that flows strictly in one direction, but our mental experience of that river is entirely up to us.

## Scene 47

By embracing curiosity, seeking out novelty, and breaking free from the shackles of routine, we can make our days feel rich, long, and full.

## Scene 48

So go ahead—live adventurously, pay attention, and make every single moment count. Thank you for watching, and stay curious.
